# 08 · Discussion

The results chapter laid out the evidence; this chapter puts it back into context — first the boundaries of this research (8.1), then a stocktaking of the checks in progress (8.2), the settled rulings and the directions not yet started (8.3), and finally the next steps (8.4).

## 8.1 Limitations

**Sample.** All comparisons rest on somewhere between single digits and a dozen-odd race rounds (the basis defined in 04.4). This caps the strength of every statistical statement, and it means the question "which model is better" can only receive a descriptive answer in most settings.

**One season, one set of regulations.** Every reading comes from the same season, the first under the 2026 regulation change. Cross-regulation generalization has not been tested at all — the "distribution shift" difficulty listed in 1.1 (regulation changes, upgrades, and seat changes diluting the transfer value of historical data) was never directly quantified inside this report; it was a real constraint while modeling, and it is one of the most important limitations of this research.

**Knock-on effects of lineup changes.** This is the largest problem the current research has not yet begun to solve. The driver changes around R12 (Hadjar's injury withdrawal, Lawson's promotion to Red Bull, Tsunoda's return) set off a three-layer chain, documented in full in 7.3: six candidates could not produce a ranking (the prediction contained Hadjar, who had already left the seat, so the entire round was unscoreable — including the two strongest candidates in the window); the incoming Tsunoda was "invisible" (he was not in the season-start scoring universe, had no features, and was necessarily missed — his actual P10 in R13 was a guaranteed absence from the scoreable candidates); the transferred Lawson was a "mismatch" (the driver-and-constructor feature combination broke, and the constructor strength basis drifted). No layer has a mechanism to handle it — the ruling and the cost are in 8.3 and chapter 09.

**Correlation between candidates.** 31 candidates yielded only 17-19 distinct top-ten orderings (6.4). "Number of candidates" is not "number of independent pieces of evidence"; it also means the ceiling on what ensembles can gain is bounded by the correlation among candidates — slot ensembles and ensembles cannot manufacture independence out of nothing.

**Overfitting risk not quantified.** The race line's test window was only two rounds (the limiting note at the end of 6.7); the qualifying line's leave-one-out validation mitigates within-window selection risk, but it is still a test on the same batch of data. The "gains" on both lines have never been through a genuinely independent significance assessment — this is the other face of the position taken in 04.4.

**Data gaps.** Tyre and pit-stop data were never collected at all — public channels do not provide per-lap tyre state or pit-stop detail, so there is no starting point even for collection — and strategy-type features therefore cannot be backtested. High-precision telemetry was not collected. The OpenF1 raw data (practice lap times, weather, sector timings) has been archived but not aggregated into usable features (the prerequisite work of chapter 03, route P2). These gaps demarcate which methods could not enter this report in the first place.

**The practice-session question** deserves its own paragraph, because it is the first thing racing fans ask. The fact, verified entry by entry on September 5: all 31 qualifying candidates use zero practice data. The community genuinely splits on whether that is right — one side holds that practice running (different programmes, fuel loads, unfamiliar parts) says nothing about qualifying, the other that this only means you have not looked properly. Both claims have since been put to the test (Chapter 09, entries 13–14): practice pace does correlate strongly with qualifying (ρ=0.886, positive in 14/14 rounds), but it is redundant with recent form (overlap 0.876), explains none of the model’s errors, and every replacement ablation fell below the +0.010 bar — the qualifying-side line closed. The kept-open race-side variant was tested the same day and closed with it: the correlation is real and the residual correlation significant (the grid-anchored race model overestimates practice-fast teams, fleet residual ρ=-0.604), but the ablation converts none of it into score (+0.0006 at best). Correlation proved not to be model increment: the C0 scale only responds near the top-ten boundary, and both signals live where it is insensitive. The models still use zero practice data, and now by evidence rather than by default. The archived practice data itself stays collected and checked weekly (8.4), kept for the review lens and the lap-time layer.

**Documentation gaps.** Two items are flagged as they are: the process by which the race line's reliability feature was abandoned has no record (7.2); the cause of the R10/R11 prediction discrepancy for #31 between the two runs has not been identified (7.3). We publish both because "process not made public" is exactly one of the gaps we criticized in 1.2 — we cannot commit that offense ourselves.

## 8.2 Checks in progress

**The blind test of the constructor strength correction.** What was pre-registered: two trigger rounds (bound to two upgrade events — the McLaren event corresponds to R14, the Aston Martin event to R15); two frozen numeric parameters (the number of gated windows k=3, the number of gradual-blending windows m=3) and one winning variant (A2 — gradual blending, as distinct from switching wholesale; full definition in chapter 09); and an adjudication threshold (only if, on the trigger rounds, the C0 score gap Δ≥+0.010 between "correction on" and "correction off" is reached does the correction mechanism go back to adjudication on "whether it enters the main line" — a ruling made by a person). Status: the R14 trigger reading has since been computed, under the current caliber (the seat-rotation policy live, the R06 revision absorbed — the pre-registered numbers of September 5 do not carry over across universes, and this run declares that openly): **exactly zero movement on every carrier metric**. The correction-on arm raised McLaren's strength value by +0.112, yet flipped no donor ranking in any pipeline, so the trigger-round delta is 0.0000 against the +0.010 bar, and the hit/miss counts are unchanged. The final verdict stays open by design: the pre-registration requires both trigger rounds, and the Aston Martin regime first triggers at R15 (Baku, September 26) — the last clean sample inside the pre-registered frame, with the verdict script prepared in advance. The zero-patch arm was verified bit-identical to the official scorecards, so the reported deltas are pure correction effects. The late registration of the R14 reading is itself part of the record, and the reader may weigh it as such. The trigger event itself has archival support (McLaren's R11 upgrade package; the event archive in 3.5). R15 has not yet been run.

**Two observations on the record.** The narrowing lead at R14 (+0.0394, the smallest of the three rounds) and the weak lower-grid push (the R12 P8–P10 gap) are both on the record under the freeze discipline of 6.8, held for later review; touching either again (adjusting weights or adding push) must go through an isolated experiment (04.6: any change after the freeze is a new experiment) and a pre-registered adjudication (the freeze–prospective protocol of 1.3), not a direct edit to the model.

**One newly opened question.** "Degree of shuffling" (how much the order swaps around) currently has only an after-the-fact proxy — inferred backward from a low baseline score — and no independent metric; whether it can be estimated before the session is also unanswered (7.2). It deserves a dedicated design: if the degree of shuffling at a given round could be estimated beforehand, the finding of 7.2 (model value moves in the same direction as the degree of shuffling) would become an actionable pre-race signal.

## 8.3 Settled rulings and directions not yet started

The four items in this section carry different statuses: the first three are final rulings (barring new evidence); the last is waiting on its preconditions.

**Name-list mechanism: none.** Patching the lineup assumption would shake the design foundation that "candidates are traceable end to end" — a score must answer "could this prediction have been made before the session", and a lineup patch is precisely the kind of thing that is hard to prove in hindsight. The cost was a loss in the comparability of the qualifying line (7.3); the mechanism of entry 12 has since resolved the scoring stoppage itself. This is a ruling made after weighing the trade-off, not an oversight. To face the three layers head-on, the direction is not to patch the old candidates (that type of "mechanism" is indeed ruled out) but to move the scoring universe from a "season-start snapshot" to a versioned dynamic lineup — a structural change that needs dedicated design and pre-registration (see item 4 in 8.4).

**Constructor strength correction: not in the main line.** The rulings from two rounds of isolated experiments — the first tested six correction variants, the second tested a "gated" variant (the correction switches on only in designated rounds after an upgrade event) — put the effect size of every correction form inside the noise band of +0.006 to +0.010 (best combination about +0.007), below the reconsideration threshold; more persuasively, the gains of the fake-event control exceeded those of the real-signal group — meaning this magnitude is indistinguishable from noise. The ruling moved the candidate into the pre-registered blind test (8.2).

**Pre-race evidence: removed from the main line.** The fifth component never scored a round (entry 15 traced why); a full reconstruction of what it would have consumed — rebuilt for twelve rounds under mechanical-inclusion and existence-proof rules, then run through correlation, combination and ablation — produced no increment (the best signal was a strength proxy; the ablation summed +2 against a bar of +3, with ΔC0 ≈ 0). The component was retired from the scoring pipeline as a verified equivalence transform, the official caliber unchanged (entry 16).

**Lap-time layer: not started (waiting on preconditions).** The precondition (feature aggregation of the OpenF1 raw data) is incomplete; the fusion constraint was also written down in advance (chapter 03): the lap-time layer may change the ordering of the scoring system's backbone only when forward validation improves both ranking quality and lap-time error at the same time.

## 8.4 Next steps

1. **Data backfill**: aggregate the archived OpenF1 raw data into session-level features — the batch of features needed to unlock the lap-time layer (the chapter 03 route P2), and the step that turns "potential features" into "usable features". The use of practice pace was tested and closed in September (Chapter 09, entries 13–14) — on the qualifying side first, on the race side the same day; the aggregation itself is retained, serving the lap-time layer.
2. **Lap-time layer**: once the data is ready, implement and test it under the fusion constraint of chapter 03.
3. **Cross-event comparison (not yet started)**: of the three-way separation established in 6.2, this is the third independent piece. Design points already reserved include: the composite score may offer a ranking recommendation, but whether a candidate is promoted from "participating in the comparison" to "main-line use" is ruled on by a person (the decision-authority clause of the scoring specification); the remaining design, such as cross-round stability, will be filled in at launch.
4. **Dynamic lineup (versioning the scoring universe)**: make transfers and new drivers characterizable and scoreable — the direct answer to the three-layer chain in 8.1; requires dedicated design and pre-registration.
5. **Estimating the degree of shuffling before the session**: the newly opened question (8.2).
6. **Close the two documentation gaps**: the keep-or-drop history of the race line's reliability feature (traced back: no explicit decision on record, 7.2) and the investigation into the cause of #31's cross-run discrepancy (requires recomputation) — until then, the main text keeps its as-is annotations.
7. **Re-evaluating the reliability feature**: it was silently skipped during the standardized rewrite (7.2), but it bears directly on "who might retire from a session" (the Verstappen example in 7.2). The follow-up is to re-evaluate it as a candidate feature — via an isolated experiment and pre-registration; in parallel, the discipline has been set that feature-set changes must leave a decision record.
8. **Starting public operation**: fixed publication of pre-session predictions and post-session reconciliation, beginning with the first race week after this report is published (the timing was set out in 7.1).

Each of these items has a home: it either enters the execution list of the next step, or waits as an open question for data, or stays shelved as a recorded ruling. The final chapter (09) gathers the key choices this report has made onto one timeline — including the original source of each of the rulings above.
